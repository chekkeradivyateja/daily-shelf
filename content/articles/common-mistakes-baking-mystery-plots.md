## The Timeline Trap

The most frequent error in baking-centered mysteries is an impossible timeline. Sourdough is slow. It requires time for fermentation, shaping, and baking. If your protagonist discovers a body in the morning and expects to have a fresh, complex sourdough loaf ready for a dinner party that same day, you have lost your reader. Novice writers often rush the process to fit the crime and the solution into a single chapter. To fix this, extend your story’s timeframe. Let the crime happen over the course of a weekend or a week. Use the waiting periods for character interaction and clue gathering. If you need a faster-baking element, introduce quick breads or yeasted rolls as secondary items, but keep the sourdough as the central, time-consuming anchor. The patience required for the bread should mirror the patience required for the investigation.

## Chemistry and Chemistry-Adjacent Errors

Another common mistake is a lack of understanding regarding the science of baking. Sourdough relies on wild yeast and bacteria. It is not a magic potion that can be added to any dough to make it sour. If your character is a professional baker, they would know that a contaminated starter smells off and tastes unpleasant, not just "sour." Misrepresenting the smell or taste of the bread can break the immersion for readers who bake. Similarly, be careful with the use of ovens. A standard home oven cannot reach the temperatures required for certain industrial processes. If your plot involves destroying evidence in the oven, ensure the temperature and duration are realistic. The bread might burn, but it won’t vaporize. Stick to plausible scenarios where the heat affects the physical state of the evidence in a visible way, such as charring or hardening, rather than magical disappearance.

## The Competent Sleuth Paradox

Many writers make the mistake of giving their amateur sleuth too much forensic knowledge. A baker is an expert in dough, not in toxicology. If your character identifies a poison by the color of the bread or the specific mutation of the yeast, ask yourself: how would they know? Unless they have a background in food science, this leaps the shark. Instead, let their expertise be in the rhythm of the bakery. They notice that the delivery schedule was wrong, that the flour bag was opened from the wrong side, or that the sourness of the starter indicates it was fed with a different water source than usual. These are observations a baker would make, not a forensic scientist. Ground their deductions in their professional experience, not in encyclopedic knowledge. By respecting the limitations of the baker’s skill set, you create a more believable and engaging narrative.
